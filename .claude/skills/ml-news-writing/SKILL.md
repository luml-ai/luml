# Reported writing on AI and machine learning

A piece in this genre is 1,200–3,000 words (median around 2,900) that earns its claims in public. Everything contested arrives attached to a named person, a dated document, or a number with its owner. The writer supplies sequence, mechanism and explanation; the prose moves on long clause-built sentences rather than on labels, fragments or cadence. Pieces under roughly 1,200 words run short, linear and headingless; pieces past 1,500–2,000 words carry a few headings and several sub-arguments.

## Voice

Third person carries the narration. Systems, researchers, labs and institutions are the grammatical subjects; you stay offstage in every reporting sentence.

Attach every evaluative claim, forecast, number and contested judgement to a named person or a quoted document in the same sentence or the next. Your unattributed voice states what happened, what a document contains, and how a mechanism works. Never write "experts say", "many researchers believe", "one prominent lab" when the holder is nameable. If you cannot name who established a figure, cut the figure.

Sets differ on how much you judge in your own voice, and the choice is governed by what you are writing. In a report of an event or episode, route every appraisal of significance through sources and keep your own verdicts to a couple of unrepeated sentences near the end. In an argued essay, state your judgements flat and unhedged — but only judgements, never facts you cannot source.

First-person plural appears in most pieces: roughly one *we / our / let's* every 250–400 words (2.4–3.7 per 1000). It means the field, the public, or the reader being walked through an explanation — never a claim of authorship or access.

First-person singular is absent from reporting sentences and always absent from the opening. Where it appears it marks a judgement or an admission of not knowing, at most once every 300–500 words, and never as a standalone sentence.

Direct address is rare: about 1 *you* per 1000 words. Use it only inside a hypothetical or worked explanation ("imagine", "let's say", "you'd consider"), and stop when the explanation stops. Never use bare imperatives to carry a step of reasoning, and never use generic "you" as a stand-in for a practitioner or for "one".

Contract routinely — *it's, doesn't, wasn't, they're* — including in flat declaratives and in the first two paragraphs.

Delete sentences that announce your interpretive procedure: "it's worth being precise about", "the honest framing is", "the real question is", "that is the actual claim". Start the explanation instead.

## Structure

Paragraph one is concrete: a dated event, a named actor, an artifact, or a hard claim with a verdict on what it does and doesn't establish. Never a definition, never background about why the field matters, never the writer. Delete any opening sentence that would still be true if the thing you are reporting had never happened.

Name the actual subject and the central claim early — the sets place the deadline between 40 and 200 words, and the shorter the piece the earlier it must land. Let no paragraph in the first 400 words be free of a proper noun, a date, or a physical particular.

Paragraph two either extends the specificity (first quotation, first numbers) or pivots to stakes or mechanism, often on a contrastive connector. Never announce the argument you are about to make.

Openings run two to six paragraphs before the first break. Carry turns in the first clause of a paragraph — a contrast, a concession, a consequence.

Endings widen from the specific case to the unresolved: what remains unknown, what might still go wrong, what someone intends to do next. Use conditional or future constructions. Never summarize, never list takeaways, never restate the opening image, never advise, never close on an aphorism or a symmetrical reversal. Whether the last sentence is a source's forward-looking quote or your own plain statement of consequence varies — a quote is the safer default when the material has people on the record; if it's yours, keep it under 25 words with no appended qualification.

## Sentences and paragraphs

Hold mean sentence length at 22–27 words. Build length by hanging subordinate and relative clauses off one main clause, so claim, caveat and interrupting aside arrive in one breath; put at least one sentence past 35 words in each substantive explanatory passage. Em dashes, semicolons and parentheticals carry glosses and qualifications.

Paragraphs run 2–4 sentences (whole-piece mean near 2.5), 40–130 words, one job each — narrate an event, or define a mechanism, or carry a source's assessment, not two. Break at a change of episode, actor or year.

Short sentences are the verdict after the mechanism, not the default. Cap sentences under 8 words at roughly one per 400–600 words, never two in a row. Write no verbless fragments.

Single-sentence paragraphs are reserved for a pivot or punchline — the sets range from at most one per piece to about one every six to eight paragraphs; the busier the piece is with headings, the fewer it needs. Never use one as a section opener.

Contrastive connectors — *but, yet, instead, meanwhile* — open sentences and paragraphs constantly and supply the forward motion.

## Texture and evidence

Anchor every paragraph of general argument to a checkable particular: a figure, a quoted line, or a named person's specific act. Never run two consecutive paragraphs without a proper noun, a number or a quotation.

Where the material gives you someone's words, quote them verbatim inside quotation marks with attribution in the same sentence — roughly one quotation of 10–30 words per 250–350 words in quotation-heavy reporting. Give every named person a tag at first mention (role plus institution, or place plus date), then surname alone. Never put quotation marks around words a source did not use. Where a claim turns on what some material actually says, show a long specimen before characterising it; adjectives about evidence do not substitute for the evidence.

Numbers appear at the precision the material gives, with unit and date, paired with a prior value or converted to human scale. Keep a source's own hedge when they hedged ("about", "more than"); do not add hedges of your own to figures that were exact, and do not compute a new ratio or total and present it as reported.

Gloss every technical term and unit at first use — inline or in the next sentence, saying what one unit physically consists of — then use it bare.

Introduce a concrete case without a frame sentence, often as a homely analogy for the technical object, and reuse it as a touchstone later. Give one or two cases an extended walk-through and dispose of the rest in a clause.

Budget one developed figure of speech per 300–700 words. Never stack two in adjacent sentences, never open or close a paragraph on one, and delete any figurative sentence that is the only explanation offered for a causal claim.

Hedge inside the sentence — *may, might, tends to, there is little evidence that* — every claim about the future, about a cause, or about what a group believes. Where a mechanism is not publicly established, say so in one sentence, then cast your reading as inference.

## Formatting

Roughly half of pieces run headingless. Under about 1,500–2,000 words, use none. Above that, use two to five in the whole piece (0.3 per 1000 words is the median, and 1.7 is the top of the normal range), with at least the first several hundred words unheaded. A heading is a 2–5 word noun phrase or compressed clause — no numbering, no colons, no question marks, sentence or title case. It marks a turn or stage; it never states the verdict of the section beneath it, and it never comments on the argument. If two sections could be joined by one transition sentence, delete the heading and write the sentence.

Cite by dense inline hyperlink on the descriptive phrase that makes the claim — roughly one link per 100–150 words — pointing at primary material. No bare URLs, no reference list.

Lists are usually absent. Where three or more genuinely parallel items appear in an argumentative passage, a list is allowed, but items are paragraph-length, not short scannable bullets.

No code, no equations, no mathematical notation; state relationships in words, with at most an italicised single variable letter. No bold in body prose. Reserve italics for a contrast between two named terms, at most twice per 2,000 words.

## What you must not do

These hold regardless of anything above. The reference writers had real
experiments, real colleagues and real citations behind their prose; you are
writing on request, and the genre's habits must not become a licence to invent.

* Never claim experience you do not have: no experiments you ran, no scenes
  you witnessed, no dated encounters, no colleagues who said something.
  Opinions, hunches and admissions of what you do not know are yours to state;
  biography is not.
* Never invent a citation, a number, a dataset size, a benchmark result or a
  quotation. If a claim needs a source you cannot name with confidence, make
  the claim smaller or drop it.
* Never stage a figure, table or code listing you cannot actually show, and
  never point to one ("as the diagram below shows").
* When the material is genuinely unsettled, say so plainly; do not backfill a
  mechanism to sound authoritative.
